One Trunk or Many?
Probing Representational Convergence in the 4M Multimodal Encoder

Martina Gatti· Albert Fares· Adrien Clement· Arthur Margeat
École polytechnique fédérale de Lausanne
Code

Using 3 modalities

Abstract

This study investigates the extent to which shared-weight multimodal models, specifically the 4M architecture, develop unified representations across disparate inputs such as RGB, depth, and surface normals. By applying a multi-metric protocol (CKA, PWCCA, and k-NN overlap) across transformer encoder layers, we analyze the geometric and topological congruence of these modalities against a null-hypothesis framework. Our findings reveal that representational similarity between paired scenes rises monotonically, becoming highly significant at deeper layers. Crucially, consistently with our definition of unification, the encoder successfully preserves modality-specific subspaces. t-SNE visualizations show that while modality clouds remain spatially distinct, their internal scene-level structures become rigorously congruent. Ultimately, our project illustrates that shared weights in this architecture do not force a spatial collapse of modalities, but rather align them into parallel, geometrically synchronized representation spaces, adding a useful perspective to the exploration of “any-to-any” models.

01Introduction & Motivation

Large multimodal models like 4M are trained to encode many modalities into one shared trunk. The design assumption is that representations converge: an RGB patch and the corresponding depth patch of the same scene end up nearby in latent space, with surface normals, segmentation maps, and other modalities folding into the same neighborhood.

This assumption is rarely tested directly. It is mostly inferred from downstream task performance, or from the model's ability to generate one modality conditioned on another. We make it empirical — opening the trunk and measuring, layer by layer, how similarly the encoder describes the same scene observed through different sensors.

Two threads of prior work motivate the question. The Platonic Representation Hypothesis argues that sufficiently scaled models converge to a shared world model independent of the surface form of their input. And CKA and related similarity probes have proven to be effective tools for unpacking representational structure inside deep networks. Combining them gives us a concrete way to ask whether 4M's "one trunk" is genuinely one.

Research questions

RQ1. Across the 12 encoder layers of 4M-21, do paired activations from different modalities of the same scene cluster more tightly than activations from mismatched scenes — and if so, where in the network does this convergence emerge and persist?

RQ2. To what degree is that convergence complete? Specifically, what fraction of the cross-modal geometric structure is linearly unified, and how much remains modality-specific (fragmented) at the deepest layer?

02Related Work

While multimodal architectures have demonstrated strong downstream performance, the internal geometry of their shared representation spaces remains underexplored. We build on several areas of prior work to investigate whether these models truly converge into a unified space.

2.1Multimodal representation learning

Large-scale models like 4M and 4M-21 [1, 2], FLAVA, and ImageBind posit a single representation space for disparate inputs. However, research into the modality gap [6] shows that models can yield representations where modalities live in distinct, near orthogonal subspaces despite paired training. Our work complements these architectures by asking whether the internal representations of a shared generative encoder genuinely converge in relational structure, rather than just inferring convergence from downstream tasks.

2.2Representational similarity analysis (RSA)

Centered Kernel Alignment (CKA) [3], PWCCA [4], and k-NN overlap are standard tools for comparing heterogeneous representation spaces. Prior work typically uses these metrics to compare different models or different layers on the same data. In contrast, we apply them to compare different modalities at each layer of the same model, utilizing a permutation null distribution to control for spurious correlations.

2.3Probing and subspace interpretability

To quantify structural fragmentation, we rely on the linear probing convention [7, 8], which conservatively bounds whether information is genuinely “present” in a representation. We combine this with subspace projection methods, such as Iterative Null-space Projection [11] and LEACE [13], which analyze residual structures after linearly erasing concepts. We adapt this logic into a cross-modal setting via transmodal triangulation, measuring how much shared structure between two modalities remains after accounting for what is linearly captured by a third.

2.4The Platonic Representation Hypothesis

Huh et al. [5] argue that sufficiently scaled models converge toward a shared statistical model of the world, a Platonic representation. Our study serves as a local, layer-wise test of this hypothesis within a single model: does a multimodal encoder trained on diverse inputs actually learn one internal language, or does it preserve modality-specific dialects throughout the trunk?

03Methodology

3.1Model, modalities, datasets

4M-21 is a multimodal foundation model developed at EPFL VILAB. It uses a shared transformer encoder that processes all input modalities — images, depth, normals, and more — in a unified architecture. The encoder has 12 layers and a hidden dimension of 768 per token.

This shared design is the core assumption our project tests: does the encoder actually learn one representation for all modalities, or does it keep them separate?

  • RGB Standard 3-channel color image, what a camera sees.
  • Depth A map of how far each surface point is from the camera.
  • Normals A map of which direction each surface patch faces in 3D space (e.g. floor faces up, wall faces sideways).

Beyond encoding each modality separately, we also run joint forward passes where two modalities are fed into the encoder simultaneously. We test three regimes:

  1. Separate passes : each modality encoded alone.
  2. Self-vs-joint : comparing a modality's representation alone vs. with a partner, to test whether the partner shifts it.
  3. Joint-vs-joint : comparing the two modality slices from one shared forward pass, to measure how unified they become when processed together.

3.3Activation geometry visualization

To complement the scalar similarity metrics, we directly visualize the geometry of the activation space at each encoder layer using dimensionality reduction. We apply PCA to 50 components as a denoising step, then run t-SNE to produce a 2D or 3D embedding. Points represent individual token activations, colored by modality, scene category, or scene identity depending on the selected granularity. This lets us see whether modality identity, scene type, or individual scene is the dominant organizing principle in the encoder's representation space at each layer.

3.4Similarity metrics

  • CKA  (Centered Kernel Alignment) : Compares two activation matrices via a kernel inner product; invariant to orthogonal transforms and isotropic scaling.
  • PWCCA  (Projection-Weighted CCA) : A canonical-correlation score in which each direction is weighted by the L1-norm of its projection onto the activations, emphasizing the directions the data actually uses.
  • k-NN overlap  : For each anchor token, fraction of its k nearest neighbours in modality A that also appear among its k nearest neighbours in modality B.

3.5Permutation null & multiple testing

For every (layer, metric, modality-pair) combination we construct a null distribution by repeatedly pairing activations from different scenes (shuffling scene identities while keeping per-modality batches intact). To avoid inflating the null with intra-category similarity, the two scenes in each shuffled pair are constrained to come from different scene categories (e.g. bathroom vs. office, drawn from Hypersim's trajectory metadata). We sample n = 1000 mismatched draws per (layer, metric, pair) and apply adaptive stopping at α = 0.01 with Benjamini–Hochberg FDR correction across the 12 layers × 3 metrics tested simultaneously. Observed paired-scene scores are reported as z-scores against this null along with BH-corrected p-values.

04Results

Three measurement layers, increasingly quantitative. We first look directly at the geometry of the activation space (§4.1–4.3) — what the modality clouds look like at each encoder layer, how same-scene paired distances evolve, and how internally diffuse each modality's cloud is. We then move to scalar similarity metrics under separate forward passes (§4.4), compare them against joint forward passes (§4.5–4.6), and finally quantify cross-modal unification through transmodal triangulation (§4.7).

4.1Activation space geometry (t-SNE)

We visualize the token activation space at each encoder layer using t-SNE (with PCA-50 pre-reduction for denoising), coloring points by modality, scene category, or scene identity. This directly reveals whether modality, scene type, or individual scene is the dominant axis of variation in the encoder's representation space at each layer — a question the scalar similarity metrics alone cannot answer.

What to look for

Under the unification definition introduced in §1, modality-specific subspaces should persist; what we expect to converge is the relational geometry within each cloud. We therefore look for matching internal organisation across clouds (same scenes pulling to matching positions on each side of the modality boundary), not for spatial fusion of the clouds themselves.

Taken together, the three t-SNE colourings build a coherent and increasingly precise picture. The modality colouring establishes that the two modality clouds remain in distinct regions of the latent space at every layer, across both datasets — consistent with the encoder preserving modality-specific subspaces alongside the shared one. The overall picture is the progressive development of matching internal structure within distinct modality subspaces — exactly the form of unification predicted by §1: shared relational geometry in a common subspace, modality-specific information preserved alongside. The similarity metrics quantify that growing correspondence; the t-SNE visualisations show what it looks like geometrically.

4.2Paired-sample distances per layer

The t-SNE figures above show how each modality's whole cloud moves. Here we measure the per-sample picture. For each scene, we compute the Euclidean distance in PCA-2 space between the representations of two modalities at each encoder layer. A decreasing median means same-scene representations from different modalities are getting geometrically closer as depth increases. The boxplots provide the quantitative geometric counterpart to the t-SNE. The modality clouds do not merge, but same-scene pairs do get closer across layers, and that convergence is statistically significant for all three pairs.

Interpretive note

The three pairs converge in distinct ways, each consistent with what the similarity metrics and t-SNE already suggested.

RGB ↔ Depth converges continuously and accelerates in the deep layers. The distance is still decreasing at layer 11, indicating the alignment continues to deepen through the final encoder layer. The encoder is systematically pulling same-scene RGB and depth representations together across the entire dataset.

RGB ↔ Normals converges slowly and modestly throughout. The shallow slope reflects the difficulty of bridging appearance and geometry. The encoder makes progress at every layer but absolute distances remain larger than for the other two pairs.

4.3Activation entropy per modality

The distance plots show how close same-scene representations from different modalities are in space. Shannon entropy asks a complementary question: how internally varied is each modality's cloud at each layer? A tight, compact cloud has low entropy — the encoder is producing similar representations for all scenes under that modality. A diffuse, spread-out cloud has high entropy — the encoder is producing more varied, discriminative representations. Beyond the per-pair distances, we ask whether the encoder also brings the internal complexity of each modality cloud closer together — i.e. whether modalities are processed with similar entropy, on top of their relational alignment.

Interpretive note

The most striking pattern across all three pairs is how compact the geometric modalities are right after tokenisation. Depth and normals enter the network as tight, low-variance clouds while RGB is already diffuse. In the first one or two layers, the geometric modalities expand rapidly — depth nearly doubles its entropy by L02, closing most of the initial gap with RGB. This early expansion is not alignment; it is transformation. The encoder is unpacking the compact geometric encoding into a richer, more distributed representation.

What happens after that expansion differs across pairs.

For depth ↔ normals, the two clouds reach similar entropy levels early and the gap remains small throughout — consistent with fast, early convergence.

For RGB ↔ depth, depth stays more diffuse than RGB through most of the network, only converging at L11 — consistent with late-accelerating alignment where the deep layers are doing the hardest work.

For RGB ↔ normals, the two modalities interleave closely from around layer 5 onward — consistent with the slow, steady convergence observed in the distance plots.

One anomaly is worth noting: depth drops sharply at L01 in the depth ↔ normals pair before recovering. This produces a brief spike in the entropy gap at that layer. A plausible explanation is that the first transformer layer temporarily compresses depth representations before expanding them again, but this does not affect the overall trajectory in any meaningful way.

Summary

Taken together, the entropy figures add a final nuance to the main question. The partial alignment we observe across the network is not just a matter of the two clouds moving closer in space. The encoder also reshapes the internal complexity of each modality's representations across layers, bringing them toward similar levels of diffuseness. Even this convergence is only partial and pair-dependent. At layer 11, each modality retains a distinct representational character: RGB remains the most varied, normals consistently more diffuse than depth, depth the most variable across datasets. The encoder brings these characters closer together while preserving the modality-specific structure expected from our unification definition.

4.5Joint processing — does feeding modalities together close the gap?

Joint processing — feeding both modalities together in a single forward pass — meaningfully increases cross-modality similarity compared to separate passes. The gap is largest for RGB ↔ Normals (CKA +0.31 on Hypersim, +0.16 on DIODE) and RGB ↔ Depth (+0.12 on Hypersim, +0.09 on DIODE). By contrast, self-vs-joint similarity stays close to 1 for RGB-involving combinations (CKA ≈ 0.999–1.000) but drops to ≈0.7–0.85 for some Depth/Normals slices in deep layers (§4.6) — the encoder integrates partner information without disrupting RGB representations, while geometric modalities are more perturbed.

4.7Cross-modal unification — transmodal triangulation (RQ2)

RQ2

RQ1 establishes that the encoder converges; RQ2 quantifies how complete that convergence is. What fraction of the cross-modal geometric structure between two modalities is linearly unified, and how much remains modality-specific?

For every triplet (Atarget, Bprojected, Cwitness) we compute a CKA fragmentation score: the fraction of the structure shared between the target modality A and the witness C that cannot be linearly recovered from B (the modality whose linear span is used for projection). A score of 0 means the full A–C linear structure is recoverable through B; a score of 1 means B is irrelevant to the A–C alignment. We report the symmetrised score per pair, averaged over the two possible projection directions (which modality is projected vs. witnessed), and test it against a permutation null (1 000 shuffled triplets, BH-FDR corrected).

Interpretive note

At layer 11, all three pairs sit far below their layer-0 fragmentation level, but not uniformly. The two RGB-involving pairs (Depth–RGB, Normals–RGB) drop sharply, ending at ≈91% and ≈87% linearly unified respectively. The non-RGB pair Depth–Normals barely moves: it starts at ≈23% fragmentation and ends at ≈19%, a drop of only ~3 percentage points compared with ~27 pp for Depth–RGB. This asymmetry is consistent with RGB acting as a geometric hub through which Depth and Normals are each indirectly aligned, rather than the two geometric modalities converging directly.

In aggregate, at the final encoder layer ≈86.5% of the cross-modal geometric structure is linearly unified under triangulation. The remaining residual fragmentation is small but statistically significant in every pair (BH-FDR p < 0.001, 1 000 permutation draws), and it does not vanish at any layer: the encoder approaches full linear unification without reaching it under linear probing.

05Discussion & Conclusion

Three complementary measurement layers point in the same direction. Similarity metrics (§4.4) show that CKA, PWCCA, and k-NN overlap rise monotonically with encoder depth and remain significantly above the permutation null at every deep layer, across all three pairs and both datasets. Activation geometry (§4.1–4.3) shows that this rise happens without the modality clouds ever merging in space — the per-modality clouds stay separated at every layer while their internal scene-level organisation becomes increasingly congruent across modalities. Transmodal triangulation (§4.7) puts a number on what fraction of the cross-modal geometric structure is linearly unified at the deepest layer.

Key takeaways

  • Progressive cross-modal alignment, not spatial collapse. Paired-scene similarity grows with depth, but the modality clouds remain separated at every layer (§4.1) and the per-sample paired distances decrease without ever closing to zero (§4.2). 4M converges modalities toward congruent internal geometries rather than a single shared region of latent space.
  • RGB acts as a geometric hub. RGB-involving pairs (Depth–RGB, Normals–RGB) unify the most strongly under triangulation (≈91% and ≈87% at layer 11), while Depth–Normals barely moves across layers (frag. only drops from ≈23% to ≈19%). This asymmetry is consistent with RGB providing a common coordinate system that Depth and Normals each get mapped into.
  • Joint processing amplifies cross-modal similarity at the slice level. When two modalities share a single forward pass, the cross-modality similarity between their two slices (joint-vs-joint) is higher than between two separate passes, particularly for the harder pairs (RGB ↔ Normals: +0.31 on Hypersim, +0.16 on DIODE). The gain is largest in deeper layers, where cross-modal attention has accumulated.
  • Self-vs-joint similarity stays near identity (CKA ≈ 1.0) for RGB-involving combinations, but drops to ≈0.7–0.85 for some Depth/Normals slices in deep layers (§4.6). The addition of an RGB partner barely perturbs a modality's own representation, while geometric modalities are more perturbed — the partner's effect manifests in both the relative joint-vs-joint geometry and, for Depth/Normals, a measurable shift of the focal slice itself.
  • DIODE values are systematically higher than Hypersim values for the same pair × metric in 7 of 9 conditions. This is likely a dataset-character effect (DIODE has lower visual diversity than Hypersim's 100 scenes across 17 categories); both datasets nonetheless yield the same monotonic depth-trend, so the qualitative finding does not depend on synthetic vs. real data.

Conclusion

Across both datasets, three metrics, and the full 12-layer depth, paired modalities of the same scene become progressively more similar in their relational structure (RQ1) without collapsing into a single spatial region — exactly as expected from our unification definition (§1). At the final encoder layer, transmodal triangulation gives an average of ≈86.5% linearly unified cross-modal structure across pairs (≥81% for every pair tested), with the residual fragmentation significant at every layer (BH-FDR p < 0.001). In the framing of our project title: 4M's encoder is one trunk in the sense that all modalities flow through shared weights into geometrically congruent subspaces — but it is a measurably imperfect one under linear probing. A small but real fraction of cross-modal structure remains linearly inaccessible from the projected modality even at the trunk's top.

Limitations

Scene diversity. Our Hypersim analysis uses 4 000 paired samples drawn from 100 indoor scenes (out of 461). Statistical estimates are stable at this sample size, but the scene diversity is bounded — all Hypersim scenes are indoor, and DIODE adds outdoors only at smaller scale. Whether the same depth-trend holds for visually further-out distributions (e.g. medical, satellite, low-light) is an open question.

Segmentation modality excluded. The only publicly released semseg tokenizer (tok_semseg) was trained on COCO-Stuff (134 classes), incompatible with Hypersim's NYU-40 label set. A Hypersim-specific tokenizer would be needed to include this modality.

Null distribution assumptions. The permutation null pairs activations from mismatched scenes of different categories but preserves within-modality statistics. If scene-level content is not the primary source of cross-modal correlation, the null may be conservative and z-scores inflated.

Geometric, not functional similarity. CKA, PWCCA, and k-NN overlap measure statistical and geometric similarity. High similarity does not imply that the encoder uses the shared representation interchangeably for downstream tasks. Cross-modal decoding probes (e.g. RGB → Depth vs. Depth → Depth reconstruction) and residual-correlation analyses would be needed to test functional compatibility; these were sketched in the proposal but left as future work.

Training exposure confound. RGB inputs are far more frequent than depth or normals during 4M training, which may itself explain RGB's apparent role as a geometric hub (§4.7). Disentangling architectural bias from training-frequency bias would require either retraining 4M with balanced modality exposure or a controlled exposure-frequency ablation.
